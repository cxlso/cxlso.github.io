Chapter 1 — Introduction

Contemporary architecture is increasingly shaped by a contradiction between technological capacity and ecological consequence. Computational design, digital fabrication, and robotic systems have expanded what architects can model, simulate, and produce, yet many of these capacities remain embedded within industrial paradigms of standardization, specialization, centralized expertise, and resource-intensive production. At a moment marked by material scarcity, climate pressure, and infrastructural inequality, the problem is not the absence of advanced tools, but the models of efficiency, repetition, ownership, and control through which those tools are organized. The epigraphs that open the dissertation frame this tension: technology has long promised liberation through knowledge, method, and mechanization, but that promise remains compromised when methods are enclosed, machines are centralized, and production reproduces dependency rather than expanding collective agency.

The design-to-fabrication continuum is proposed as a response to this condition. Rather than treating design and fabrication as separate phases, it defines a feedback-based framework in which computational models, material behavior, machinic constraints, ecological conditions, and shared technical knowledge continuously inform one another (Figure 1.1). Computation is therefore positioned neither as an autonomous generator of form nor as a neutral instrument of optimization, but as a mediating infrastructure through which design intent, matter, machine operation, environmental information, and human participation can be coordinated. This framework provides the conceptual and methodological basis for investigating how computational and robotic fabrication might become more open, transferable, situated, and ecologically accountable.

1.1 Research Motivation

The research begins from the premise that design can operate as more than the production of objects. It can reorganize relations among knowledge, matter, technology, and collective life. Buckminster Fuller’s planetary and comprehensive approach to design is formative in this regard. His work connected technical invention to the finite and interdependent condition of the Earth, while locating wealth less in the ownership of matter than in the capacity to understand and transform material processes responsibly (Fuller, 1969). This position suggests that design should be evaluated not only through the objects it produces, but through the systems of knowledge, coordination, circulation, and adaptation that it makes possible. The geometric and structural study shown in Figure 1.2 exemplifies this integration of dimensional calculation, construction logic, formal reasoning, and material organization.

Technological development, however, does not inherently expand agency. Industrial production repeatedly separated conception from execution, expert knowledge from operational labor, and production from its ecological consequences. Simone Weil’s critique of industrial labor identifies alienation as an epistemic condition as well as an economic one: participation remains limited when people encounter only the results of a process and not the methods through which it is organized (Weil, 1934). A comparable division persists in contemporary computational and robotic fabrication when advanced processes remain enclosed within specialized laboratories, proprietary software ecosystems, machine-specific protocols, or workflows dependent on tacit expertise.

The ecological dimension of this separation became increasingly visible through systems analyses of industrial growth. By modeling the interdependence of population, industrial output, resource depletion, food production, and pollution, The Limits to Growth demonstrated that technological development cannot be evaluated independently from planetary feedback and finite resources (Meadows et al., 1972). Its relevance here is methodological as well as environmental: computation can reveal relations that remain obscured when production is treated as a sequence of isolated operations. Yet modeling ecological limits from a distance is insufficient if material sourcing, fabrication energy, machine infrastructure, waste, maintenance, and local resource conditions remain external to the design workflow.

The resulting motivation is neither technological rejection nor technological solutionism. Computational design and robotic fabrication can extend industrial abstraction when they depend on standardized materials, closed interfaces, centralized expertise, and controlled environments. They can also support a different model of practice in which material variability becomes a source of design information, machines are made more intelligible, environmental conditions enter the workflow as active parameters, and technical methods remain open to adaptation. The research investigates the technical, methodological, and infrastructural conditions under which this second trajectory becomes possible.

The continuum is developed through a cumulative sequence of workflows rather than through a single comprehensive system. The breathing modular wall translates passive environmental intelligence into parametric geometry, simulation, assembly logic, and fabrication preprocessing. The topology-derived rib system examines how computational complexity can be materialized through accessible two-dimensional CNC cutting, adaptive kerfing, bending, nesting, and assembly. The irregular-timber workflow integrates photogrammetry, digital cataloguing, structural feedback, calibration, and robotic milling to treat non-standard material geometry as an operative input. The final robotic workflow connects scanning, mesh-to-toolpath modeling, robotic sequencing, live machine communication, and graded non-planar deposition. The two interludes examine bounded morphogenesis and open-source robotic infrastructure as conceptual and technical conditions linking these experiments.

These investigations are not presented as definitive technological solutions. They function as methodological probes into how computational models, fabrication machines, materials, environmental information, and participants can modify one another within a structured process. Their value therefore lies in both the fabricated outcomes and the transferable knowledge produced through scripts, toolpaths, calibration methods, hardware systems, documentation, and workshop-based testing.

The broader motivation is a shift from control toward stewardship. Greater technical sophistication is insufficient when the resulting workflows remain opaque, environmentally detached, or inaccessible beyond the conditions in which they were produced. A design-to-fabrication framework becomes consequential when its operations can be understood, questioned, modified, and adapted to other materials, machines, and contexts. The research therefore treats intelligibility, transferability, and ecological negotiation as constitutive properties of fabrication practice rather than as supplementary forms of dissemination or evaluation.

1.2 Computational Design and Fabrication Today

During the past three decades, computational design and digital fabrication have shifted architectural computation from representation toward operation. Digital models now participate in the organization of geometric relations, material systems, environmental analyses, fabrication sequences, robotic motion, and construction logic. Architectural authorship consequently extends beyond the delineation of static form to the design of procedures through which geometry, matter, performance, and machine behavior are related.

This disciplinary development has been advanced through research environments including ICD/ITKE Stuttgart, Gramazio Kohler Research at ETH Zurich, The Bartlett, the Architectural Association, and CITA Copenhagen. Their work has expanded robotic fabrication, material computation, bio-inspired construction, generative systems, and feedback-based design methods (Figure 1.3). Collectively, these trajectories demonstrate that computation can participate directly in materialization, allowing fabrication constraints and material behavior to inform design rather than remaining downstream technical concerns.

Digitally controlled fabrication also alters the industrial relationship between variation and production. Industrial mass production depended on identical repetition because molds, dies, tooling, and production lines made standardization economically advantageous. Variable digital information allows differentiated components to be generated and fabricated without requiring an entirely separate production system for every variation, supporting mass customization and non-standard assembly (Carpo, 2017). This capacity does not automatically overcome industrial logic, but it weakens the assumption that material production must be organized around sameness.

The contemporary condition can consequently be understood as postdigital. Computation is no longer a discrete novelty or aesthetic category; it has become an ordinary infrastructure through which information, labor, material processes, and fabrication systems are organized (Negroponte, 1998). The relevant question is therefore not whether architecture is digital, but how computational infrastructures structure the passage between design information and material realization. Neri Oxman’s material ecology extends this shift by treating computation as a medium for coordinating material composition, structural behavior, environmental performance, and fabrication across scales rather than as an abstract generator of geometry (Oxman, 2012).

Despite these advances, computational design and fabrication remain unevenly accessible. Many workflows depend on highly specialized expertise, expensive laboratory infrastructures, proprietary software, standardized material assumptions, and controlled fabrication environments. A project may achieve geometric or material sophistication while its underlying methods remain difficult to reproduce, maintain, or adapt outside the institution in which they were developed. Technical capacity has therefore expanded more rapidly than methodological transferability.

Robotic fabrication makes this discontinuity particularly visible. Robotic arms, sensing systems, and custom end-effectors can connect digital models to cutting, deposition, assembly, scanning, or material manipulation. However, this connection depends on knowledge distributed across design software, plug-ins, post-processors, controller languages, network protocols, calibration procedures, end-effector design, safety requirements, and material-specific settings. Design intent may pass through several environments before reaching the machine, and each translation can introduce information loss, abstraction, or dependency on inaccessible expertise.

The disciplinary challenge is therefore not simply to produce more complex geometries or more automated processes. It is to construct workflows capable of coordinating computational ambition with material availability, machine behavior, environmental conditions, fabrication access, technical literacy, and local adaptation. The design-to-fabrication continuum addresses this challenge by treating these factors as co-defining inputs to architectural production rather than as restrictions encountered after a design has been completed.

1.3 Research Problem and Dissertation Position

The research problem emerges from the coexistence of expanded technical capacity and limited methodological transfer. Computational tools, environmental simulation, digital fabrication, and industrial robotics have increased architecture’s capacity to produce non-standard, adaptive, and materially responsive systems. Yet the workflows enabling these outcomes are often project-specific, difficult to access, and difficult to adapt beyond specialized research environments. Powerful techniques do not necessarily become shared methods.

This limitation is simultaneously technical, methodological, ecological, and infrastructural. Standardized materials, stable laboratory conditions, proprietary platforms, specialized machinery, and expert control are frequently treated as neutral prerequisites. Material availability, cultural knowledge, environmental conditions, fabrication access, energy demand, and maintenance are then introduced as external concerns rather than as active parameters capable of shaping the design process. The resulting work may be optimized within a controlled technical system while remaining poorly aligned with the situated conditions under which architecture is produced and maintained.

The central gap is therefore not an absence of innovation in computational design, material research, environmental simulation, robotics, or open-source fabrication. It is the absence of an accessible and transferable framework capable of connecting these domains as a coherent operational system. Existing projects often demonstrate advanced capacities without fully exposing how their methods can be adapted, what assumptions their workflows embed, how they respond to different materials or infrastructures, or how their technical knowledge can circulate beyond the original research environment. This reinforces a division between experimentation and dissemination, technical expertise and broader architectural agency.

The design-to-fabrication continuum is positioned as a response to this gap. It replaces the assumption of a one-directional sequence—from design, to modeling, to toolpathing, to fabrication, to evaluation—with a feedback structure in which design intent, material behavior, machine logic, environmental information, and shared methods can modify one another. Computation mediates these exchanges by translating heterogeneous inputs into operational relations. Its role is therefore neither to eliminate uncertainty nor to impose a single optimized solution, but to make the relations among design, matter, machine, and environment explicit enough to be tested and adjusted.

Methodological transferability is treated as a criterion of technical significance. A workflow contributes more than an artifact when its underlying logic can be understood, modified, reused, taught, and extended under different material and infrastructural conditions. This requires attention to the procedures through which an outcome becomes possible: scanning protocols, parametric models, simulation assumptions, toolpath strategies, calibration processes, hardware interfaces, fabrication tolerances, material feedback, versioning, and documentation. Open-source release alone does not guarantee transferability; the method must be sufficiently legible and modular for others to appropriate rather than merely reproduce it.

Ecological reasoning is likewise embedded within the organization of the workflow rather than appended as a final performance criterion. Material choice, resource availability, passive environmental principles, fabrication energy, waste, transport, maintenance, and adaptation to local constraints affect how computational and machinic systems should be designed. The research does not claim that computation resolves ecological problems autonomously. It examines how computational systems can make ecological relations and trade-offs more explicit, operational, and available for negotiation.

Robotic fabrication provides a critical site for this inquiry because it concentrates both flexibility and enclosure. Industrial robots can perform diverse material operations and can be reconfigured through custom tools, sensing, and programming. At the same time, their use often depends on machine-specific protocols, proprietary interfaces, specialist knowledge, and costly infrastructures. The developed workflows therefore investigate open and adaptable methods for toolpathing, end-effector integration, irregular material processing, scanning, calibration, controller communication, and live feedback as steps toward repositioning robotic systems as shared research infrastructure rather than elite instruments of production.

The dissertation advances the position that computational fabrication should be evaluated by its capacity to coordinate situated relations and circulate methods, not only by the formal complexity, precision, or efficiency of its outputs. The design-to-fabrication continuum provides a framework for this evaluation by connecting design, matter, machine, ecology, and technical knowledge within an open field of feedback. Its purpose is not to remove technical complexity, but to make complexity more intelligible, adaptable, and accountable to the material and infrastructural conditions in which fabrication occurs.

1.4 Research Questions

The research is guided by one main question:

How can a design-to-fabrication continuum be developed as an open, transferable, and ecologically situated framework for computational and robotic fabrication?

Five supporting questions define the principal dimensions of the inquiry.

Question 1 — Computation as Mediating Infrastructure

How can computation mediate among design intent, material behavior, machinic logic, and ecological constraints within a continuous design-to-fabrication process? Which forms of feedback allow computation to move beyond representation, automation, or isolated optimization?

Question 2 — Situated Workflows and Material Variability

How can computational workflows integrate resource availability, fabrication access, and material variability from the beginning of the design process? Under what conditions can locally available, irregular, or non-standard materials operate as design inputs rather than as deviations from standardized production?

Question 3 — Open-Source Robotics and Transferability

Which methods, protocols, interfaces, and forms of documentation can make robotic fabrication more intelligible, adaptable, and transferable? How can the demystification of robotic systems extend architectural agency beyond specialized laboratories and expert-only workflows?

Question 4 — Experimental Workflows as Methodological Probes

How can experimental fabrication projects function as cumulative investigations rather than isolated artifacts? How can each workflow test a distinct limit of the design-to-fabrication continuum while contributing methods and evidence to a broader framework?

Question 5 — Participatory and Ecologically Accountable Fabrication

Under what conditions can open, adaptable, and feedback-based fabrication workflows support greater participation, material responsiveness, and ecological accountability? What limitations remain when these workflows depend on specialized machines, global supply chains, and uneven technical access?

1.5 Research Objectives

The overall objective is to develop, test, and articulate the design-to-fabrication continuum as a methodological framework for open, transferable, and ecologically situated computational and robotic fabrication. The research integrates computation, material behavior, machinic operation, environmental information, and knowledge circulation within a cumulative experimental structure.

Objective 1 — Define the Design-to-Fabrication Continuum

Establish the theoretical and methodological distinction between a linear design-to-production pipeline and a feedback-based continuum. Clarify how computation can mediate among design intent, material behavior, machinic constraints, ecological conditions, and shared technical knowledge.

Objective 2 — Develop Situated Computational Workflows

Construct workflows that integrate environmental performance, resource availability, material variability, fabrication constraints, and assembly logic from the early stages of design. Test how computational processes can respond to passive environmental systems, non-standard geometries, and locally specific material conditions.

Objective 3 — Test Robotic Fabrication as Open Infrastructure

Develop adaptable methods for robotic toolpathing, end-effector integration, controller communication, sensing, calibration, and material processing. Document these methods through shared protocols and open-source resources so that their logic can be inspected, modified, and extended.

Objective 4 — Use Experimental Workflows as Cumulative Probes

Evaluate the framework through a sequence of projects addressing ecological translation, bounded morphogenesis, accessible CNC fabrication, irregular timber, open-source robotic integration, and situated feedback. Identify how methods, technical constraints, and failures can be transferred across experiments rather than remaining project-specific.

Objective 5 — Assess Transferability and Ecological Accountability

Evaluate the workflows according to their material responsiveness, environmental relevance, intelligibility, adaptability, and capacity to support learning and appropriation. Distinguish demonstrated environmental or technical performance from broader claims that require additional empirical validation.

1.6 Research Contributions

The research produces six interrelated contributions to computational design, robotic fabrication, and practice-based architectural research.

Contribution 1 — A Conceptual Framework for the Design-to-Fabrication Continuum

The continuum reconceives design and fabrication as interdependent states within a feedback-based structure rather than as successive phases. It positions computation as the mediating infrastructure through which design intent, material behavior, robotic constraints, ecological conditions, and shared technical knowledge can be coordinated and revised.

Contribution 2 — A Cumulative Methodological Model for Research-Through-Making

The experimental workflows operate as related probes that progressively construct and test the framework. Their sequence connects environmental translation, morphogenetic exploration, material bending, irregular timber, robotic infrastructure, and situated feedback. The methodological contribution lies in treating the workflow, its revisions, and its failures as research outputs alongside the resulting artifacts.

Contribution 3 — Situated Workflows for Ecological and Material Negotiation

The developed methods integrate passive environmental principles, simulation, resource awareness, irregular geometry, material behavior, fabrication constraints, and assembly logic. They demonstrate how computational design can incorporate non-standard or locally available conditions as operative inputs, while also identifying where environmental and material performance remains dependent on further physical testing.

Contribution 4 — Open-Source Robotic Fabrication Methods

The technical contribution includes adaptable approaches to end-effector integration, controller and signal-flow organization, robotic toolpath generation, photogrammetric scanning, calibration, non-planar extrusion, irregular-material processing, and live machine communication. These methods reduce discontinuities between design environments and fabrication systems and provide reusable technical foundations for teaching and research.

Contribution 5 — Documentation and Pedagogy as Forms of Evaluation

Workshops, courses, GitHub repositories, diagrams, Grasshopper definitions, hardware documentation, and fabrication protocols are treated as mechanisms through which workflow legibility can be tested. Participant use provides evidence about where a method can be followed or adapted and where it remains dependent on tacit expertise. This establishes transferability and appropriation as evaluative criteria within practice-based fabrication research.

Contribution 6 — An Ecological and Civic Repositioning of Computational Fabrication

The continuum situates ecological accountability within the organization of materials, machines, energy, methods, access, and maintenance rather than limiting it to formal performance or material substitution. It frames computational fabrication as a practice of stewardship and shared technical agency while acknowledging the institutional, economic, and supply-chain dependencies that constrain complete decentralization. The contribution is therefore a qualified model for more materially responsive and participatory fabrication, not a claim that technological openness alone resolves ecological or social inequality.

1.7 Dissertation Structure

The dissertation is organized into three parts that move from theoretical framing, through methodological and experimental development, to critical synthesis and conclusion.

Part I — Theoretical and Methodological Framework establishes the intellectual and methodological basis of the research. Chapter 1 defines the problem, the design-to-fabrication continuum, the research questions, the objectives, and the principal contributions. Chapter 2 develops the historical and theoretical foundations through industrial production and ecological limits, computational design and digital fabrication, open-source and distributed production, and post-anthropocentric design. It then distinguishes a feedback continuum from a linear pipeline, defines computation as a mediating infrastructure, and identifies the unresolved gap in accessible integrated workflows. Chapter 3 presents the practice-based methodology through which experimentation, prototyping, workshops, documentation, open-source dissemination, and cumulative evaluation construct and test the framework.

Part II — Experimental and Theoretical Investigations develops the continuum through four principal workflows and two interludes. Chapter 4 translates the passive cooling logic of the Iranian windcatcher and qanat into a computational modular wall integrating environmental simulation, assembly, and fabrication preprocessing. Interlude I examines morphogenesis as a bounded field in which emergent possibility is conditioned by structural and material viability. Chapter 5 develops topology-derived bending-active rib structures through accessible CNC cutting, adaptive kerfing, nesting, and assembly sequencing. Chapter 6 engages irregular timber through photogrammetry, digital cataloguing, computational joinery, structural feedback, calibration, and robotic milling. Interlude II examines end-effectors, controllers, signal flow, toolpath libraries, GitHub documentation, and replicability as components of an open-source robotic technical commons. Chapter 7 integrates scan–probe registration, direct mesh-to-toolpath modeling, real-time robot communication, robotic sequencing, and graded non-planar deposition within a situated fabrication loop.

Part III — Synthesis, Discussion, and Conclusion interprets the cumulative findings and delimits their implications. Chapter 8 compares the experimental workflows to examine the continuum as a methodological reorientation grounded in bounded negotiation, material accountability, access, appropriation, technical commons, protocol sovereignty, and the ecological contradictions of computational fabrication. It also considers decentralized indexing and constrained artificial intelligence as conditional horizons rather than demonstrated outcomes. Chapter 9 answers the main research question, consolidates the validated contributions, states the limitations of the research, and identifies concrete priorities for empirical testing, technical development, infrastructural transfer, and governance.

The sequence moves from conceptual definition to methodological testing and from experimental evidence to critical interpretation. The chapters and interludes are therefore cumulative rather than independent: each contributes a specific relation—environmental, computational, material, machinic, infrastructural, or pedagogical—to the broader inquiry into how computational robotic fabrication can become more open, transferable, materially responsive, and ecologically situated.
