Chapter 4 — Ecological Intelligence and Vernacular Computation

The experimental sequence begins with the translation of vernacular environmental intelligence into a computational design-to-fabrication workflow. Rather than starting from robotic automation or formal complexity, the project examined here begins from a passive cooling system whose performance depends on the interaction of air, water, geometry, thermal mass, pressure, and climatic context. The breathing modular wall reinterprets the environmental logic of the Iranian windcatcher and qanat as a modular envelope system integrating parametric modeling, environmental simulation, assembly design, support generation, and non-planar robotic extrusion (Urroz & Yousif, 2024).

The project does not treat vernacular architecture as a formal catalogue to be reproduced. It approaches it as procedural knowledge accumulated through long-term interaction among climate, material, construction, and occupation. Computation is used to make selected relations explicit, variable, and testable while acknowledging that a digital model cannot reproduce the full cultural, environmental, and material complexity of the precedent. The workflow therefore investigates ecological translation: how inherited environmental principles can inform contemporary geometry and fabrication without being reduced either to visual imitation or to a claim of universal optimization.

4.1 From Vernacular to Computational Logic

Contemporary computational design often presents simulation and optimization as primary routes toward environmental performance. Such methods are valuable, but they can imply that environmental intelligence originates within the model and is subsequently applied to architecture. Vernacular systems suggest a different sequence. Their environmental logic is developed through prolonged adjustment to climate, material availability, construction practice, maintenance, and occupation. Computation enters not as the origin of that knowledge, but as a medium through which selected relations can be described, varied, and reconsidered under contemporary conditions.

Ecological and vernacular systems share a reliance on distributed feedback. Biological processes respond through local material interactions rather than through a single centralized objective. Roots, fungi, airflow, moisture, and thermal exchange develop through continuous adjustment to surrounding conditions (Andréen & Goidea, 2022). Vernacular construction operates at a cultural and temporal scale through a comparable accumulation of feedback. Orientation, ventilation, shading, thermal mass, water systems, courtyards, material assemblies, and maintenance practices are refined through repeated use rather than invented as isolated technical devices.

This correspondence should not collapse biological and cultural systems into one category. Biological adaptation and inherited construction knowledge operate through different agencies, timescales, and forms of responsibility. Their methodological relevance lies in a shared departure from one-directional command. Both demonstrate that environmental performance can be embedded in relations among material, form, climate, and practice rather than added after a design has been completed. Cultural intelligence can therefore be understood as environmental knowledge extended through collective memory, while biological systems reveal how adaptation can emerge through distributed interaction (Goidea et al., 2022; Watson, 2019).

The central computational operation is translation rather than imitation. A formal imitation of a windcatcher might reproduce its silhouette while excluding the movement of air, the relation to water, pressure differences, thermal mass, and the spatial organization that enable cooling. Translation instead identifies operative relationships and asks how they might be reorganized within another architectural condition. In this project, airflow, water movement, pressure, evaporation, surface curvature, cavity depth, porosity, and assembly orientation become interdependent variables within a parametric system.

Figure 4.1 represents this shift from environmental precedent to computational relations. The diagram does not claim to capture the windcatcher and qanat in their entirety. It selects principles that can be associated with geometric and environmental parameters and carried forward into the modular wall. This abstraction is productive because it enables comparison and iteration, but it remains partial because cultural use, construction material, regional climate, maintenance, and full-scale thermal behavior exceed the computational representation.

Simulation consequently performs a translational and pedagogical role. Environmental relationships historically refined through long periods of use can be examined through rapid geometric variation. Apertures, cavity forms, module orientations, and assembly configurations can be changed and compared without requiring a full physical reconstruction for every iteration. This accelerates design inquiry, but it does not establish that computational simulation supersedes the empirical knowledge embedded in vernacular practice.

The distinction is important because the environmental behavior of a passive system remains contingent on climate, material properties, water availability, construction tolerance, occupation, and maintenance. A model can clarify selected tendencies and expose assumptions, but it cannot independently establish the performance of a built wall under specific conditions. Computation therefore supports a dialogue between inherited environmental knowledge and contemporary fabrication rather than replacing one with the other.

The workflow establishes ecological translation as the first operational condition of the design-to-fabrication continuum. Environmental principles inform computational variables; those variables generate a family of components; simulation provides feedback on selected environmental tendencies; and fabrication preprocessing tests whether the resulting geometry can be constructed. Environmental reasoning is therefore introduced before material execution, not added as a final performance check.

4.2 Operationalizing Vernacular Passive Cooling

The project responds to a broader architectural condition in which environmental regulation is frequently separated from the design of the envelope. Heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning systems can compensate for buildings whose geometry and material organization were not developed to mediate climate. Environmental correction is then displaced into equipment, ducts, compressors, sensors, and control systems installed after fundamental spatial and material decisions have been made.

This separation reflects the linear logic criticized in the theoretical framework. Architectural form is conceived first, while environmental performance is treated as a downstream engineering problem. Mechanical systems are indispensable in many climatic and programmatic conditions, and vernacular precedents cannot simply replace them. The relevant issue is that reliance on active conditioning can externalize environmental intelligence from architecture and reduce the envelope to a sealed boundary whose performance depends primarily on attached technical infrastructure.

Energy use in buildings reinforces the need to reconsider this relation. Buildings account for a substantial proportion of energy consumption in developed countries, with heating, cooling, ventilation, and air-conditioning forming major demands (Pérez-Lombard et al., 2008). Improving equipment efficiency remains necessary, but it does not by itself address the organizational separation between form, climate, and environmental regulation (Belmonte et al., 2021). Passive design strategies instead coordinate orientation, shading, air movement, humidity, thermal mass, water, and material assembly as part of the architectural system from the beginning.

Vernacular architecture provides a broad repertoire of such integrated strategies, including wind-driven ventilation, courtyards, shading, earth sheltering, water systems, and thermally massive construction (Cojocaru & Isopescu, 2021). These precedents should not be romanticized as universally sustainable or transferred without regard for their original climate and culture. Their relevance lies in demonstrating a different epistemic structure: environmental behavior is embedded in spatial and material organization rather than appended as an independent service.

Recent research has shown how parametric modeling and simulation can support the contemporary study of passive systems by making climatic relationships available for iterative analysis (Abdelmohsen et al., 2019; Caetano & Leitão, 2020; Kolarevic & Malkawi, 2005). Material-based computation and low-tech digital fabrication likewise demonstrate that sophisticated environmental reasoning does not require a simple opposition between ancestral knowledge and advanced technology (Antonini et al., 2020; Yazici & Tanacan, 2020; Yuan et al., 2013). Computation can help clarify and adapt low-tech principles when its role remains responsive to their material and climatic origins.

The selected precedent combines the Iranian windcatcher, or badgir, with the qanat. The windcatcher receives prevailing air through elevated openings and directs it through internal channels. In configurations connected to a qanat, moving air interacts with an underground water channel and with cooler surfaces before entering the occupied space. Pressure differences, thermal mass, convection, and evaporation contribute to air movement and cooling (Liu et al., 2024). The effectiveness of the system depends on the relation among orientation, wind direction, opening geometry, channel dimensions, water, temperature, and the spatial organization of the building.

The windcatcher–qanat assembly can therefore be understood as an environmental apparatus. It does not calculate digitally, but it organizes climatic forces through form and matter. Air is captured, redirected, accelerated, cooled, and distributed through the calibrated relation of architectural elements. Its intelligence is relational rather than concentrated in one component.

The breathing modular wall transfers this horizontal and vertical infrastructure into the thickness of an envelope. In the traditional configuration, an air-catching tower is connected to interior spaces and, in some cases, to an underground water channel. In the proposed system, the relationship is rotated and compressed. A vertical water channel forms the environmental core of the wall, while a porous exterior cap receives and guides air toward that core (Figure 4.2).

This transformation is more than a change in orientation. It converts an environmental relation distributed across tower, building, ground, and underground infrastructure into a modular envelope system. The wall is conceived as an active climatic zone rather than a sealed separator. Exterior air, interior space, water movement, solar exposure, and material mass are coordinated through its thickness.

The project does not claim that this compression preserves every behavior of the original system. The qanat's underground thermal mass, extended air path, climatic context, and relation to settlement cannot be reproduced simply by inserting water into a wall. The design instead isolates selected principles—air capture, directed flow, geometric constriction, water contact, evaporation, and shading—and reorganizes them as a testable architectural proposition.

Through this operation, vernacular knowledge becomes computationally actionable. Airflow, porosity, cavity shape, curvature, water circulation, and assembly orientation can be described as parameters and examined through simulation. Environmental analysis enters during design development rather than after the module has been formalized. The resulting wall is therefore not a copy of a historical type, but an experiment in transferring environmental relationships into a contemporary design-to-fabrication system.

4.3 The Breathing Modular Wall as Computational Environmental System

The environmental proposition required a modular system rather than a singular façade object. Modularity allowed the project to coordinate component-scale geometry with wall-scale aggregation, environmental variation, fabrication feasibility, transport, connection, and assembly. The unit could remain repeatable enough to be produced through a shared workflow while retaining enough geometric variation to generate different spatial and environmental configurations.

Each block consists of two primary parts: a core and a façade cap. The core contains the vertical water channel derived from the qanat principle. The cap forms the porous, wind-catching surface and directs exterior air toward the core. This division allows the environmental roles of water circulation and air capture to be geometrically coordinated while remaining identifiable within the assembly.

The relation depends on a 90-degree rotation of the traditional windcatcher–qanat arrangement. The vertical water channel becomes embedded in the wall, while the air-catching surface is distributed across the façade. The module thereby transforms wall thickness into an environmental interface in which air movement, water, shading, and material mass can be related through geometry.

A 30 × 30 cm base unit was selected as a manageable scale for parametric development, environmental simulation, connection design, and fabrication study. The dimension provided sufficient space for internal cavities, a water channel, interlocking interfaces, fasteners, and façade articulation while permitting the blocks to aggregate into larger assemblies. Five module types were generated from this common base.

The five types form a controlled vocabulary rather than a collection of unrelated alternatives. Shared dimensions and connection logic preserve compatibility, while differences in orientation and curvature allow the wall to change direction and produce varied spatial configurations. Repetition therefore occurs at the level of method and interface, while differentiation occurs at the level of geometry and aggregation.

A 30-degree bend provides the principal geometric variation (Figure 4.3). The angle allows visible curvature to develop across an assembly without producing excessive deformation within each block. Because 30 degrees divides evenly into 360 degrees, the same logic can support repeated, symmetrical, and circular configurations. The choice connects formal variation to modular coordination rather than treating curvature as an unconstrained aesthetic operation.

The block system therefore operates through compatible differentiation. A flat, vertically curved, horizontally curved, or more dynamically articulated wall can be assembled from the same family of components. These configurations alter not only appearance but also orientation toward sun and wind, surface exposure, cavity alignment, and spatial enclosure. Modularity becomes an environmental and constructive instrument rather than a mechanism for identical repetition.

Assembly logic was incorporated during geometric development. Convex and concave interfaces at the top and bottom guide stacking and alignment. Mechanical fastening uses two M10 × 60 mm socket-head screws for vertical connections and one M10 × 40 mm screw for horizontal connections. A tab system connects three consecutive vertical blocks and distributes stability across neighboring components (Figure 4.4).

These details connect digital variation to constructability. The modules do not remain independent geometric studies; their curvature, orientation, and environmental cavities must coexist with interfaces that permit alignment, fastening, disassembly, and larger-scale aggregation. The connection system also establishes a degree of reversibility compared with permanently bonded assemblies, although its structural adequacy would require physical testing at the intended material and scale.

The façade cap includes porous openings and small overhangs. The overhangs were initially introduced to protect openings from rain and reduce unwanted lateral airflow. Their later role in solar analysis demonstrates how one geometric feature can participate in several systems simultaneously: weather protection, airflow control, shading, and formal articulation. The project therefore avoids treating environmental devices, constructive details, and visual geometry as entirely separate layers.

Internal cavities continue across adjacent modules, producing a connected zone for air movement and thermal exchange. Their geometry is intended to guide air from the exterior surface toward the water core. At the wall scale, orientation and module type determine how these cavities align and how the environmental interface changes across the assembly.

The parametric model coordinates block dimensions, curvature, porosity, cavity form, connection interfaces, orientation, and aggregation. This coordination represents an early form of operational integration in the dissertation. Environmental principles are not yet validated at full scale, but they are carried into the geometry and construction logic of the system rather than remaining an external conceptual reference.

4.4 Environmental Simulation as Translational Feedback

Environmental simulation was introduced to examine whether the geometric translation retained meaningful relationships to solar exposure and airflow. It was not used to certify performance or identify one universally optimal wall. The analyses compared tendencies among configurations and identified where geometric decisions could be revised or retained (Zhong et al., 2019).

Four wall configurations were assembled from the five block types (Figure 4.5). Their vertical, horizontal, and combined curvatures provided different orientations and surface conditions for analysis. These configurations demonstrate the capacity of a limited component family to produce distinct wall geometries, but they also provide a basis for testing how aggregation changes exposure and airflow.

Solar radiation analysis focused on the relation between façade geometry and direct exposure. The small overhangs originally developed for rain protection and airflow control produced shaded regions on portions of the porous surface (Figure 4.6). Their environmental role therefore extended beyond their initial constructive purpose.

This result is important at the level of workflow logic. The shading feature is embedded in the module and therefore carried through fabrication rather than added later as an independent device. A local geometric decision participates simultaneously in weather protection, porosity, and solar moderation. The simulation makes that relation visible and allows the feature to be compared across different aggregations.

The analysis does not, however, establish passive heat dissipation or a quantified reduction in wall or air temperature. Solar exposure is one factor among material absorptance, thermal mass, conductivity, orientation, climate, and water behavior. The simulation therefore provides design-stage evidence of shading tendencies rather than proof of thermal performance.

Airflow analysis examined the intended path from the porous façade through the module cavities and toward the water core. The cavity geometry was shaped to receive and direct air rather than permit undifferentiated permeability. A cone-shaped constriction was intended to increase local air velocity and lower static pressure in accordance with the Venturi principle.

The Venturi relation is used here as a geometric design hypothesis rather than as a measured result. The simulation visualizes airflow through the constricted geometry, but the project did not experimentally quantify pressure differential, velocity increase, flow rate, or the influence of turbulence and surface roughness. The cone therefore identifies an intended mechanism that requires further computational and physical validation.

The water channel establishes the potential for convective and evaporative exchange as air approaches the core (Figure 4.7). The environmental concept assumes that moving air and water can contribute to thermal moderation, but the magnitude of that effect depends on air temperature, relative humidity, water temperature, flow rate, contact area, material behavior, and residence time.

The airflow study therefore supports a limited conclusion: the geometry organizes a directional pathway between exterior openings and the internal core. It does not independently demonstrate a temperature decrease. Measured cooling performance would require physical prototypes and controlled testing of airflow velocity, pressure, humidity, water behavior, and thermal conditions.

Together, the solar and airflow studies demonstrate how simulation can function as translational feedback. Environmental relationships are made visible at a stage when geometry and aggregation can still change. The analyses identify secondary effects of constructive details and expose assumptions that require later validation. They do not collapse the design into a single performance optimum, and they preserve the possibility of alternative configurations.

Relative to the methodological criteria established in Chapter 3, the simulation stage provides evidence of feedback responsiveness because environmental analysis informs the interpretation of module geometry. Its situated ecological relevance remains partial because the simulations are not calibrated to a specific full-scale construction, material system, or occupied climatic scenario. The workflow therefore moves environmental reasoning upstream while keeping the limits of its evidence explicit.

4.5 Fabrication Preprocessing and Constructability

After environmental analysis, the workflow shifted from geometric and climatic exploration toward fabrication preprocessing. This stage examined whether the façade cap could be translated into support geometry, robotic paths, calibration procedures, and a coordinated production sequence. Constructability was treated as part of design development rather than as a downstream rationalization of a completed form.

The façade cap presents a specific fabrication problem. Its curvature and porous cavity geometry are poorly suited to conventional horizontal slicing. Planar layers would introduce stepped surfaces, alter the intended orientation of openings, increase material accumulation in some regions, and disconnect deposition from the geometry through which airflow is organized. A non-planar strategy was therefore developed so that the toolpath could follow the panel surface more directly.

Two operations structured the process: the generation of a matched support and the generation of non-planar robotic toolpaths. The support was necessary because the extruded material required a stable substrate corresponding to the panel curvature. Rather than depositing over a conventional flat print bed, the process used a temporary form shaped to each façade variation.

In Grasshopper, an array of points was projected onto the target panel surface. These points were used to generate a patch approximating the required support geometry (Figure 4.8). Each of the five façade variations could therefore produce a corresponding substrate through the same parametric procedure.

The support was conceived for fabrication through a conventional desktop 3D printer before being fixed to the robotic workbench. This separates temporary support production from final material deposition. Desktop printing provides a relatively accessible means of producing the geometric substrate, while the robotic arm performs the larger-scale or materially specialized extrusion.

This separation demonstrates simplexity within the workflow. The robotic system is not required to produce every element. Each fabrication process is assigned the operation for which it is comparatively suited. At the same time, the support introduces additional material and fabrication time, which must be considered in any later ecological assessment. Reuse, material choice, and recovery of temporary supports would affect the overall environmental consequence of the process.

Once positioned on the workbench, the physical support must be registered with the computational model. Calibration aligns the robot coordinate system, tool frame, support location, and generated path. A positional mismatch can cause tool collision, displaced deposition, inconsistent bead geometry, or cumulative error. Calibration therefore performs a critical mediating role between digital geometry and the situated fabrication environment.

The second operation is the generation of non-planar toolpaths in Grasshopper. Unlike conventional slicing at constant heights, the paths follow the panel and support geometry in three-dimensional space. Changes to the panel, path spacing, layer organization, or material settings can be propagated through the parametric definition rather than requiring the sequence to be reconstructed manually for every component.

The five façade variations share a common toolpathing logic while producing geometry-specific trajectories. This is an important form of transferability within the experiment. Standardization occurs at the level of procedure and data structure, while differentiation remains possible at the component level. The workflow therefore demonstrates how a method can remain repeatable without requiring identical parts.

The deposition sequence begins with five horizontal contour layers spaced 5 mm apart. These layers establish a solid base, improve contact with the support, and stabilize the beginning of the print. A nominal layer height of 7 mm defines the broader vertical increment of material deposition.

From the sixth layer onward, the path changes to vertical concentric contours (Figure 4.9). These paths reduce material accumulation and fabrication time relative to a fully filled component while preserving the porous organization of the cap. The two path regions respond to different requirements: the base prioritizes stability and adhesion, while the vertical contours prioritize lightness, porosity, and material economy.

The stated parameters should be understood as workflow settings rather than validated universal values. Their suitability depends on nozzle diameter, bead width, extrusion rate, material rheology, travel speed, support geometry, drying or curing behavior, and the scale of the final component. They document the logic of the developed sequence but would require recalibration for another material, machine, or environmental condition.

Path thickness was visualized to approximate the width of the extruded bead. This allowed the designer to inspect spacing, overlap, coverage, and potential interference between adjacent trajectories before execution. The path therefore operated as more than an abstract robot curve; it became a preliminary representation of deposited material volume.

Six-axis robotic motion permits the extruder to approach the support according to the local orientation of the path rather than remaining vertical over a horizontal bed (Mitropoulou et al., 2025). This expands the range of surface conditions that can be addressed and allows deposition logic to respond more directly to the intended geometry. It also introduces additional constraints involving tool orientation, collision, reachability, gravity, bead adhesion, and changes in material flow.

The broader production sequence was organized through a dual-arm simulation using two UR10e robots coordinated in Grasshopper. The first arm extrudes a panel onto the calibrated support. The second removes the completed component and transfers it to an organizer rack so that the extrusion process can continue with another unit (Figure 4.10).

The simulation decomposes fabrication into support placement, calibration, extrusion, part removal, storage, and preparation for assembly. Each operation requires a different spatial and temporal logic, but all are represented within the same computational environment. Grasshopper therefore coordinates geometry, path generation, and robotic sequence rather than functioning only as a form-generation tool.

The dual-arm study should be understood as a production scenario rather than a validated autonomous cell. Successful implementation would require reliable gripping, release from the support, collision avoidance, synchronization, material curing sufficient for handling, safety logic, and controller-level communication. The simulation establishes the choreography and identifies dependencies, but it does not demonstrate uninterrupted physical production.

Fabrication preprocessing consequently provides evidence of operational integration at the level of computational workflow. Geometry, support production, registration, path generation, layer strategy, and robot sequencing are connected. Full constructability remains only partially demonstrated because material performance, structural adequacy, release behavior, dimensional tolerance, long-duration extrusion, and full-scale assembly require physical validation.

The workflow is nevertheless methodologically legible. Support generation, path organization, layer parameters, and robotic roles are sufficiently explicit to identify what another implementation would need to preserve and what would require recalibration. Its transferability lies in the structure of the method rather than in the assumption that the same settings will perform identically across all materials and machines.

4.6 Contribution to the Design-to-Fabrication Continuum

The breathing modular wall contributes a model of ecological translation. A vernacular passive-cooling precedent is not copied as an architectural image but rearticulated as a set of relations among air, water, geometry, porosity, solar exposure, assembly, and fabrication. Computation makes these relations explicit enough to generate alternatives and connect them to environmental analysis and production planning.

The workflow also demonstrates operational integration across several scales. Environmental principles inform module geometry; module geometry informs wall aggregation; simulation identifies shading and airflow tendencies; assembly details constrain the components; support geometry and non-planar paths translate the façade cap toward fabrication; and robotic sequencing coordinates the proposed production cycle. The contribution lies in maintaining these relations within one workflow rather than resolving each as an isolated technical task.

The feedback achieved in this experiment remains partial. Simulation informs the interpretation of geometry, but no measured environmental data are returned from a fabricated full-scale wall to update the computational model. Similarly, fabrication preprocessing defines a coherent route toward robotic production, but the dual-arm sequence and final material system require further physical testing. The project therefore establishes a foundation for later chapters, where scanning, material variability, calibration, and live robotic feedback become more directly integrated.

Its ecological relevance also requires qualification. Translating a passive strategy and reducing material through porous non-planar paths do not by themselves establish sustainability. Water use, support production, material composition, energy demand, durability, maintenance, and climatic suitability remain part of the unresolved assessment. The chapter's contribution is methodological rather than a claim of proven environmental performance: it demonstrates how ecological knowledge can enter the design-to-fabrication process early enough to shape geometry, assembly, and fabrication while preserving the need for contextual and empirical validation.
