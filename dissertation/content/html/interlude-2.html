Interlude II — Open-Source Robotic Ecologies as Technical Commons

Robotic fabrication remains culturally and technically associated with the industrial environments in which robots became familiar: automotive assembly lines, conveyor sorting, electronic-component production, palletizing cells, and increasingly automated “dark factories” organized around repetition, speed, controlled conditions, and minimal human presence. In these settings, robots operate behind guards, proprietary controllers, and specialized technical procedures. The separation is therefore both physical and epistemic: people are distanced not only from the moving machine, but also from the protocols that organize its actions.

The interlude develops from Open-Source Approaches to Integrating Advanced Robotic Systems in Architecture (Urroz & Yousif, 2025). It approaches robotics from the opposite direction, asking how robotic systems can move beyond the factory and proprietary laboratory into shared design and fabrication cultures where their methods can be understood, modified, and appropriated for local use. Physical access to a robotic arm is insufficient when the relations among design software, robot programs, controller signals, end-effectors, electronics, toolpaths, materials, and calibration remain opaque. The central concern is therefore robotic integration as a technical commons: a documented and extensible infrastructure through which fabrication knowledge can circulate beyond one machine, project, or research group.

II.I From Robotic Fabrication to Robotic Infrastructure

Industrial robots are frequently described as fabrication devices: programmable arms capable of executing precise spatial movement. Architectural research has used this capacity for additive manufacturing, milling, cutting, assembly, scanning, and material deposition. Yet evaluating robotics primarily through the artifact or toolpath can conceal the infrastructural chain that makes fabrication possible.

That chain includes the environment in which geometry is modeled, the procedure that converts geometry into target planes, the simulation and post-processing system, the robot controller, the physical and electrical interface to the tool, the calibration of the tool center point, the organization of analog and digital outputs, the material-delivery system, the safety setup, and the documentation that allows these relations to be reconstructed. A sophisticated prototype can therefore coexist with an opaque method.

Opacity is not only a consequence of technical difficulty. It is produced when operational relations are hidden behind manufacturer-specific controllers, closed software, undocumented workarounds, and laboratory expertise (Calixto & Croffi, 2024). A designer may generate geometry and preview robot motion while remaining dependent on a technician to assign I/O, configure an end-effector, translate program syntax, or recover from controller faults. The user has access to the output but only partial access to the means through which the output is produced.

This condition echoes the distinction between results and methods in critiques of industrial alienation. Technical freedom requires more than receiving the products of a system; it requires sufficient understanding to inspect and intervene in the process that organizes material action (Weil, 1934). Within robotic fabrication, demystification is therefore operational. It depends on access to scripts, wiring, signal assignments, calibration procedures, controller assumptions, and the reasons behind each fabrication decision.

Personal and distributed fabrication expanded access to laser cutters, CNC routers, and desktop 3D printers by combining shared machines with documentation and pedagogical frameworks (Gershenfeld, 2008). Industrial robots have entered universities and fabrication laboratories, but their availability has not automatically produced the same degree of appropriation. A robot may be physically present while remaining technically enclosed by proprietary interfaces, safety requirements, controller options, and fragmented software–hardware integration.

The open-source framework examined here treats the robot as one component within a broader ecology of software, controllers, end-effectors, toolpath definitions, electronic interfaces, and repositories (Urroz & Yousif, 2025). The infrastructure becomes transferable when these relations are exposed and organized so that they can be reconfigured for another material, tool, robot, or fabrication task.

This does not imply that every component must be open source in isolation. Industrial robot controllers and some programming environments remain proprietary. The practical objective is to increase the portion of the workflow that users can inspect and modify: the tool design, mechanical mount, actuation logic, Grasshopper definition, microcontroller code, I/O mapping, wiring, fabrication parameters, and documentation. Openness therefore operates within a partially closed ecosystem rather than outside industrial infrastructure altogether.

To treat robotics as infrastructure is also to emphasize circulation. A custom extruder should not remain an undocumented attachment; it should become a reproducible mechanical and electronic assembly. A Grasshopper definition should not remain an opaque file; it should become an annotated procedural template. A controller should not remain an improvised box understood only by its designer; it should expose how power, signal conversion, drivers, motors, valves, and the robot I/O are connected.

The shift from isolated fabrication to infrastructure changes the research output. The artifact remains important, but it is accompanied by a grammar of action: reusable relations among geometry, motion, actuation, and material behavior. These relations are the basis on which later users can test, modify, and extend the work.

II.II End-Effectors, Controllers, and Signal Flow

The robotic arm rarely acts on material without an attached tool. Extruders, grippers, routers, scanners, valves, pumps, and sensors determine what the machine can do and which material processes it can engage. The end-effector is therefore not a secondary accessory. It is the interface where computational commands, robot motion, electronic control, and material behavior converge.

This interface is also where architectural robotic workflows often become fragmented. Geometry may be generated in Rhino and Grasshopper, robot movement simulated through one plug-in, and tool activation controlled separately through a teaching pendant, relay, microcontroller, or manual switch. When these layers are not synchronized, fabrication requires improvised timing and depends heavily on operator familiarity. The framework developed through the research organized the relationship as a visible signal chain (Figure II.I).

Grasshopper contains the geometry, ordered targets, tool orientations, speeds, and actuation states. The robot controller executes the spatial program and changes its outputs at specified targets. Where the output is not directly compatible with the tool, an Arduino-based controller converts or reformats the signal. A driver, relay, or solenoid then activates the physical device.

The exact signal path depends on the end-effector. For the stepper-driven WASP extruder tested on a UR10e, the generated URP program assigned two 0–5 V analog outputs: AO0 for pulse or speed information and AO1 for direction. An Arduino read these signals on analog pins and converted them into digital pulse and direction outputs for the external stepper driver. The AccelStepper library supported control of the NEMA 17 motor (McCauley, 2010).

The Arduino was necessary because the robot output and stepper-driver input did not use the same signal format. The robot supplied an analog command, whereas the motor driver required timed digital pulses and a digital direction state. Signal conversion allowed the extrusion rate and direction to be embedded in the robot program rather than controlled independently before execution.

The pneumatic gripper required a different pathway. The MHC2-25D gripper was connected to a five-way, two-position solenoid valve. A digital tool output from the UR10e supplied the open/closed command directly to the valve. Because the voltage and logic were compatible, no intermediary microcontroller was required. The valve redirected compressed air between the two gripper states.

These examples demonstrate that the framework is modular but not electrically universal. Every integration still requires verification of voltage, current, grounding, output type, pin assignment, driver requirements, and fail-safe behavior. A 0–5 V analog command, a 24 V digital output, pulse/direction logic, and a pneumatic valve cannot be connected interchangeably. Reuse depends on understanding which part of the signal chain remains constant and which part must be adapted.

The same logic was applied to several end-effectors, including clay and thermoplastic extruders, vacuum and pneumatic grippers, a handheld router mount, and a combined clay-extrusion and scanning tool (Figure II.II). Their mechanical forms and material processes differ, but each requires a coordinated relation among robot motion, mounting geometry, signal timing, and physical actuation.

The natural-material and thermoplastic extruders used the same basic control logic but required different peripheral systems. Both relied on auger mechanisms driven by stepper motors. The clay system required pressure and consistency control, while the thermoplastic pellet extruder required heating, temperature regulation, and fan control. The controller architecture could be shared, but material-specific power and process requirements remained distinct.

The custom controller box consolidated these relations (Figure II.III). Its enclosure used laser-cut acrylic side panels, 3D-printed corner brackets, and an internal frame for the components. The principal elements included a 24 V power supply, Arduino board, 24-to-12 V step-down converter, and an external stepper driver where required. The thermoplastic version added dedicated motor power and heating control.

The 24 V supply supported devices such as the NEMA 17 motor and cooling fans, while the step-down converter supplied a compatible voltage to the Arduino. The controller boxes were designed to be mounted near the robot base to shorten wiring, maintain a compact setup, and allow the same control principle to be adapted across extrusion tools.

The unified controller reduced dependence on manually activating an extruder before the robot program began. Synchronization made operations such as extrusion jumps, start–stop control, rate variation, and retraction possible within the same fabrication sequence. The difficulty was not that these operations were theoretically new, but that previous implementations were often poorly documented or distributed across separate technical systems.

The gripper required a custom mechanical mount joining the robot flange, pneumatic cylinder, solenoid valve, and interchangeable fingers. The fingers were informed by the Fin Ray effect, in which a compliant structure bends toward an applied load and conforms around an object. Initial PLA prototypes provided lightweight adaptive contact; PETG or TPU offered possible routes toward greater toughness or flexibility. The open-source gripper was intended for light manipulation rather than as a substitute for high-force industrial tooling. This distinction is important because low cost and replicability do not imply equivalent payload, durability, repeatability, safety certification, or service life. Making the limitations visible is part of the technical commons.

Signal flow is therefore not an auxiliary electronic detail. It determines whether a geometric target can become extrusion, gripping, release, heating, scanning, or cutting at the correct moment. The end-effector is a technical hinge: it connects software to hardware and generalized robot motion to a material-specific operation.

II.III Toolpath Libraries and Shared Methods

Toolpaths organize robotic action in space and time. They contain coordinates and orientations, but also speed, approach, retraction, discontinuity, actuation, material continuity, and sequence. Sharing a robot program without exposing these assumptions communicates only the final command set, not the method through which it was constructed.

The framework therefore treated toolpathing as a library of reusable fabrication behaviors. Programs were generated in Grasshopper and compiled through Robots by Visose (Soler & Huyghe, 2016). Comparable robot-specific workflows can be developed through KUKA|prc by Johannes Braumann, HAL, FUROBOT, and other programming environments. The relevant contribution is not allegiance to one plug-in, but the organization of motion and end-effector behavior within one inspectable computational interface.

Robots was selected because it connects Grasshopper geometry to robot-specific programs through an open repository and a comparatively transparent component structure. KUKA|prc offers a mature interface for KUKA systems, while HAL and FUROBOT provide related approaches to simulation and program generation. ROS and toolkits such as BIRT and COMPAS Fab further demonstrate the broader importance of reusable control and fabrication libraries (Pacheco, 2024; Quigley et al., 2009; Van Mele et al., 2019).

In the developed Grasshopper interface, actuation commands are assigned to the same targets that define robot movement (Figure II.IV). This eliminates the need to operate the tool through a separate manual sequence and makes timing visible before execution.

A comprehensive set of definitions was developed for natural-material and thermoplastic extrusion. The library included extrusion jumps, six-axis non-planar extrusion, variable speed control, path offsets, overlap adjustment, and start–stop compensation (Figure II.V).

Extrusion jumps permit the nozzle to move between separated curves while the material command switches off and on. The definition adds adjustable offsets or overlaps near the start and end of a path because physical flow does not respond instantaneously to a command. Pressure, auger filling, viscosity, hose compliance, and nozzle resistance can delay extrusion after activation and prolong discharge after deactivation. The overlap parameter is therefore a compensation for material and mechanical latency rather than a purely geometric value.

Six-axis non-planar extrusion uses manually or automatically generated slicing surfaces and converts their normals into tool-center-point orientations. The robot can therefore maintain a changing relation to a curved substrate or deposited surface instead of reproducing conventional horizontal layers. Mesh-based inputs can also be used, although noisy normals, open contours, and abrupt changes may require smoothing and threshold controls.

Variable speed control responds to localized differences in layer height or compression. The definition maps the changing value to robot or extrusion speed and displays it as a color gradient in Grasshopper. The visualization allows the user to inspect where speed changes occur before sending the program.

This coordination differentiates robotic extrusion from simply transferring a three-axis printer path to a larger machine. Six-axis motion makes tool orientation and tool–substrate relation part of the path (Mitropoulou et al., 2025). At the same time, greater freedom introduces new constraints involving joint limits, singularities, collision, cable routing, nozzle angle, material adhesion, and gravity.

Extrusion actuation was represented through analog values from 0 to 5. A value above zero at an initial target began the material command; intermediate values changed the requested rate; and a final zero stopped extrusion. The number represented a controller command rather than a guaranteed volumetric flow. Physical output still depended on motor calibration, pressure, temperature, material rheology, and delay.

Gripping used digital states such as 0/1 or open/closed. Both analog and digital outputs could be overridden through the teaching pendant during testing. The override preserved operator intervention when calibration, unexpected resistance, or material behavior required the automated sequence to be interrupted.

A separate definition organized pick-and-place operations (Figure II.VI). The robot began at a home target with the gripper open, moved to a first waypoint, and closed around a wooden dowel. A second waypoint rotated the dowel by 15 degrees to align it with the receiving hole. The robot then moved along the dowel axis toward the insertion target before opening the gripper to release the part.

The sequence demonstrates that an assembly path is not merely a list of positions. It coordinates approach direction, jaw state, component orientation, insertion vector, release timing, and collision clearance. These procedural relations can be retained as a template even when the component geometry and final task change.

Comments, groups, naming conventions, color coding, and modular organization convert tacit fabrication knowledge into editable definitions. Users can begin from an operational example, identify dependencies, test parameter changes, and combine portions of the library. This structure reduces the need to reconstruct common operations from an empty definition.

A library does not remove the need for testing. Robot models, post-processors, controller options, material systems, and end-effectors differ. The value of a shared method lies in making assumptions explicit enough that another user can identify where translation is required. Toolpath libraries support cumulative research when later users can correct, extend, and return modified definitions rather than repeatedly rebuilding the same behaviors.

II.IV GitHub, Documentation, and Replicability

The publication of files does not by itself create an open robotic ecology. A repository without dependency lists, controller requirements, I/O mappings, diagrams, examples, calibration instructions, or troubleshooting notes may remain as opaque as a proprietary workflow. Transfer depends on whether another user can understand how the parts relate and where the setup must be modified.

Git and GitHub provide useful infrastructure for this process because changes can be versioned, compared, branched, and recombined. A workflow can be copied into a new context, revised for another robot or tool, and returned with a traceable history. The repository therefore functions as a living technical document rather than a terminal archive.

The repositories developed through the research documented MDPH2 integration on a UR10e, the WASP Clay Kit on a UR10e, robotic gripping, and robotic printing (Urroz, 2024a, 2024b, 2024c, 2024d). Together, they expose relationships among Grasshopper definitions, robot programs, end-effector mounts, microcontroller code, wiring, controller components, and material procedures.

Copying in this context is not identical reproduction. A workflow normally requires translation to another robot model, software version, tool geometry, workspace, safety protocol, material, or user skill level. Technological and architectural methods have historically developed through copying and modification across contexts rather than through isolated invention (Diamond, 1997; Leach, 2016). Version control makes this translation visible.

Three levels should therefore be distinguished. Availability means that files can be accessed. Replicability means that the documentation is sufficient for another user to reconstruct the tested setup with comparable behavior. Appropriation means that the user can modify the method for another purpose and understand the consequences of that modification. The repositories establish availability and provide conditions for replication and appropriation; independent uptake would be required to verify the latter two fully.

Hardware documentation requires more than CAD files. Users need component lists, expected performance, cost, tolerances, power requirements, and known limitations. The open-source gripper was therefore compared with commercial alternatives according to stroke, estimated grip force, and approximate component price at the time of documentation.

The open-source prototype offered an 80 mm stroke at an estimated USD 55 component cost. Its reported 50 N grip force was lower than those of the commercial tools, and the prototype did not provide the same certification, durability, sensing, repeatability, or support. The comparison therefore indicates a different design position: an adaptable and inexpensive tool for lightweight experimentation, not a direct substitute for an industrial gripper.

Transparent limitations support informed appropriation. A user can determine whether the prototype is appropriate, change the finger geometry, substitute locally available components, or redesign the actuation system. Without performance and cost information, the same CAD files would be difficult to evaluate.

Documentation also gives disciplinary visibility to minor but decisive procedures: assigning analog and digital outputs, defining a TCP, matching voltage levels, setting pulse and direction pins, compensating for extrusion delay, ordering targets, wiring drivers, configuring pressure or heating, and recovering from faults. These operations are often omitted because they appear too specific for conceptual discourse, yet they determine whether the fabrication workflow operates.

The educational testing reported with the framework reinforced this point. Undergraduate students with beginning-to-moderate Grasshopper experience used the workflows to develop robotically extruded wall modules. The exercises indicated that the general structure could be followed with instruction, while participant feedback identified the need for clearer parameter descriptions and stronger explanations of the transitions among design, simulation, and fabrication. This provides formative evidence of legibility, not a controlled or statistically generalizable usability study.

Open-source dissemination changes the researcher's responsibility. A workflow must be diagrammed, annotated, versioned, and explained beyond the author's tacit familiarity. Useful repositories include installation and dependency notes, robot and controller specifications, mechanical files, wiring diagrams, I/O tables, code comments, example definitions, calibration procedures, operating limits, safety cautions, and troubleshooting records.

Replicability should not be understood as identical output. Different laboratories use different robots, controllers, materials, tools, and safety systems. A replicable workflow is one whose logic is sufficiently exposed for another user to reconstruct or translate it. The repository must therefore communicate relationships and reasons, not only results.

The postdigital condition makes this responsibility increasingly ordinary rather than exceptional (Cramer, 2014). Computation, scripts, and networked repositories are embedded in everyday design practice. The important question is how these methods are governed and circulated. Documentation is the mechanism through which robotic knowledge can remain inspectable and open to revision.

II.V Transition: From Open Protocols to Adaptive Feedback Systems

The technical commons developed across the interlude establishes a shared operational basis for robotic fabrication. End-effectors translate robot motion into material action; controller logic synchronizes spatial and electrical behavior; toolpath libraries preserve reusable fabrication procedures; and repositories make the relationships among these elements available for inspection and adaptation.

These components extend the design-to-fabrication continuum from theory into technical practice. They allow users to trace how a design parameter becomes a target, how a target acquires an actuation command, how the controller interprets that command, and how the end-effector acts on matter. Legibility increases the capacity to intervene because the workflow can be modified at several connected levels rather than accepted as a closed service.

Chapter 7 activates this infrastructure through more dynamic communication among the computational environment, the robot, sensors, and material systems. Robot state, scanning, calibration, program execution, and tool actuation remain connected across successive operations. This structure supports more interactive and evolutive workflows in which users can observe, interrupt, recalibrate, resume, and extend fabrication as it unfolds.

The transition is therefore from documented integration to situated operation. Shared protocols provide the components and relationships; dynamic communication allows those relationships to participate in an ongoing process. The result is an increased capacity for appropriation: the user can understand not only how a workflow was assembled, but also where and when it can be adjusted during fabrication.
